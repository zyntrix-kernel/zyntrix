   257→   257→# 03. BUILD A REAL 3D CAMERA SYSTEM
   258→   258→
   259→   259→Create a dedicated cinematic scene architecture.
   260→   260→
   261→   261→The exact implementation is your decision after examining the repository, but the system must support these capabilities.
   262→   262→
   263→   263→### A. A persistent real 3D world
   264→   264→
   265→   265→Prefer one coherent WebGL world for connected cinematic scenes when practical.
   266→   266→
   267→   267→Avoid repeatedly destroying and recreating the renderer between acts.
   268→   268→
   269→   269→Use actual Three.js geometry, materials, lights, cameras, and world-space transforms wherever genuine three-dimensional rendering is required.
   270→   270→
   271→   271→React Three Fiber is appropriate if it integrates cleanly with the existing React and Next.js stack. A direct Three.js implementation is also acceptable if it provides a clearer, more reliable architecture.
   272→   272→
   273→   273→Use a client-only rendering boundary where necessary. Keep server rendering and initial content stable, and do not allow the introduction of WebGL to cause hydration failures.
   274→   274→
   275→   275→### B. An authored camera rail
   276→   276→
   277→   277→Create a timeline or camera-path system with named waypoints, explicit durations or scroll ranges, controlled interpolation and documented transitions.
   278→   278→
   279→   279→The camera should support:
   280→   280→
   281→   281→- Dolly in and out.
   282→   282→- Lateral tracking.
   283→   283→- Vertical movement.
   284→   284→- Orbital movement around important objects.
   285→   285→- Changes of elevation.
   286→   286→- Carefully limited camera roll.
   287→   287→- Target tracking.
   288→   288→- Smooth changes of composition.
   289→   289→- Transitions between close-up detail and wide establishing shots.
   290→   290→
   291→   291→Use an appropriate interpolation strategy for each movement. Catmull-Rom or another spline approach may suit spatial paths, while carefully authored curves or quaternion interpolation may be better for other transitions.
   292→   292→
   293→   293→Do not allow spline overshoot to send the camera through unrelated objects or produce accidental framing.
   294→   294→
   295→   295→The camera path should have a clear visual intention at every major point.
   296→   296→
   297→   297→### C. Scroll as a cinematic transport
   298→   298→
   299→   299→Use normalized journey progress and carefully authored timeline segments.
   300→   300→
   301→   301→For each segment, define:
   302→   302→
   303→   303→- Entry and exit progress.
   304→   304→- Camera position and target.
   305→   305→- Camera orientation and relevant framing parameters.
   306→   306→- Important object transforms.
   307→   307→- Lighting changes.
   308→   308→- Scene events.
   309→   309→- Text and interaction timing.
   310→   310→- Transition conditions.
   311→   311→
   312→   312→Support bidirectional scrubbing.
   313→   313→
   314→   314→At any point in the cinematic journey, scrolling backward must reproduce the corresponding previous state without accumulated drift.
   315→   315→
   316→   316→Avoid building a new collection of competing per-component scroll hooks for every animated object.
   317→   317→
   318→   318→Use one authoritative progress source and derive all cinematic state from it wherever practical.
   319→   319→
   320→   320→The animation must be deterministic, seekable and safe to render again after resizing or rehydration.
   321→   321→
   322→   322→### D. Camera choreography, not camera noise
   323→   323→
   324→   324→Use the camera to reveal information.
   325→   325→
   326→   326→Begin with strong compositions and controlled movement. Change distance when scale matters. Orbit only when the object benefits from being seen from another angle. Use foreground objects to create depth and spatial reveals.
   327→   327→
   328→   328→Create moments when the camera settles before the next important event.
   329→   329→
   330→   330→Avoid continuous motion that gives the visitor no opportunity to appreciate the composition.
   331→   331→
   332→   332→Use inertia and easing sparingly. Scroll input itself should remain responsive.
   333→   333→
   334→   334→**The goal is not maximum camera movement. It is maximum meaning per camera movement.**
   335→   335→

   365→   365→# 05. MATERIALS, LIGHTING AND OPTICAL QUALITY
   366→   366→
   367→   367→A real 3D renderer is only the beginning. The world needs an internally consistent visual system.
   368→   368→
   369→   369→Develop a lighting setup with a clear purpose.
   370→   370→
   371→   371→Explore:
   372→   372→
   373→   373→- Broad key lighting.
   374→   374→- Restrained rim lighting.
   375→   375→- Carefully controlled emissive elements.
   376→   376→- Environment-aware reflections.
   377→   377→- Contact shadows.
   378→   378→- Material-specific highlights.
   379→   379→- Screen emission and surrounding light spill.
   380→   380→- Atmospheric separation.
   381→   381→- Intentional color-temperature contrast.
   382→   382→- Realistic differences between metal, glass, polymer and display surfaces.
   383→   383→
   384→   384→Use proper physically based materials where appropriate.
   385→   385→
   386→   386→Distinguish surfaces through roughness, metallic response, normal detail, surface color and lighting, rather than coloring every object differently.
   387→   387→
   388→   388→Avoid making every material equally reflective. A few carefully chosen reflective surfaces will give the environment more depth than a world where everything is polished glass.
   389→   389→
   390→   390→Consider a subtle filmic tone-mapping approach and restrained post-processing where the renderer supports it.
   391→   391→
   392→   392→Depth of field must have a clear subject. Motion blur must correspond to actual movement. Bloom should emphasize controlled light sources rather than washing out the frame.
   393→   393→
   394→   394→Do not introduce a post-processing stack merely to advertise that one exists.
   395→   395→
   396→   396→Where high-end effects are too expensive or incompatible with the performance budget, design the scene so that its composition remains excellent without them.

   489→   489→Do not combine multiple scene frameworks without a concrete justification.
   490→   490→
   491→   491→Organize the resulting code around a maintainable scene architecture. Separate camera timelines, reusable geometry, material definitions, lighting, scene orchestration, data bindings, and functional interface components.
   492→   492→
   493→   493→Avoid the architecture of one enormous TSX file that contains every object, shader, camera transform and UI label.
   494→   494→
   495→   495→Use explicit types and well-defined data contracts.
   496→   496→
   497→   497→Keep all real theme values connected to the existing HCT engine. Scene-specific presentation logic may transform those values artistically, but must not replace the theme engine.
   498→   498→
   499→   499→Ensure proper resource ownership and cleanup for:
   500→   500→
   501→   501→- WebGL renderers and contexts.
   502→   502→- Geometry and materials.
   503→   503→- Textures and render targets.
   504→   504→- Resize observers.
   505→   505→- Event listeners.
   506→   506→- Animation frames.
   507→   507→- Timelines and subscriptions.
   508→   508→- Audio resources, if retained.
   509→   509→- Temporarily loaded or generated assets.
   510→   510→
   511→   511→Avoid unnecessary allocation inside the rendering loop.
   512→   512→
   513→   513→Reuse geometry and materials when appropriate. Use instancing when it genuinely improves repeated-geometry performance.
   514→   514→
   515→   515→Load expensive assets lazily when suitable. Prevent WebGL initialization from blocking essential page content.
   516→   516→
   517→   517→Use explicit rendering and quality strategies appropriate to the actual implementation. Adaptive pixel ratio, reduced effects on weaker hardware and a static fallback are preferable to pretending every device can render the same scene at the same cost.
